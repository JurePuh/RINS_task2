\section{Introduction}

This report covers Task 2 of the RINS 2026 project, which combines perception,
navigation and decision logic into one pipeline running across two rooms.

\subsection{The Task}

The robot starts in the \textbf{first room} and drives through it looking for people.
When it finds someone, it greets them and asks for a task.

\paragraph{The four tasks.}
A person hands out one of four jobs: count \textbf{rings}, inspect \textbf{barrels}, or
detect \textbf{anomalies} on the red or the green cell. Counting rings means remembering
every ring seen in the first room together with its color. Inspecting barrels means
remembering every barrel, its color and whether it lies horizontal, then driving closer
to any horizontal one to check it for a leak; a leaking barrel is announced out loud.
Detecting anomalies means driving to the belt with the matching colored line, raising
the camera, and inspecting every tile for defects.

\paragraph{The no go zone.}
A yellow line in the first room marks a no go zone. The robot must never cross it, which
constrains every path it plans there.

\paragraph{The second room.}
Once the first room is searched and the assigned tasks are done, the robot moves to the
entrance of the second room, picks up a blue line, and follows it through its
intersections until it reaches the \textbf{CTO}, who stands next to the line.

\paragraph{The report.}
Reaching the CTO triggers the report. It gives the ring count and the per color counts;
a table of every barrel with its color, a horizontal flag and a leaking flag, plus a
picture of each leaking barrel; and a table of every inspected tile with a defect flag,
plus the tile image and its anomaly mask for the defective ones. Each task also records
who asked for it.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Face Detection}

\textbf{YOLO} runs the whole image through a single network pass and returns boxes, each
with a class, a confidence and image coordinates. That single pass is what lets the
robot detect faces while it keeps moving. We use the box only as a pointer: it tells us
where the face sits in the image, and we read the matching \textbf{depth} to lift that
pixel into a 3D position on the map. The position is what the rest of the system needs,
not the picture.

\subsubsection{Calculating the Face Destination}\label{sec:facedest}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.30\textwidth}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.28\textwidth]{\dummyfig}
  \caption{Direct approach fails at a shallow angle.}
  \label{fig:direct_fail}
\end{wrapfigure}

To start a conversation the robot must stop in front of a face. Our first goal was a
point 40\,cm ahead of the face along the \emph{robot to face line}. Head on that works,
but at a shallow angle it drops the goal beside the face or into the wall: the robot
stalls and rarely ends up turned at the person (Figure~\ref{fig:direct_fail}). The
quantity that fixes this is the \textbf{wall normal}, so the rest of this section
computes it and turns it into a goal.

\paragraph{Wall normal.}
We tried the lidar first, and it was too fragile near corners and partial occlusions.
Instead we read the wall from the \textbf{occupancy grid} the robot already keeps for
navigation, whose cells are \emph{free}, \emph{occupied} or \emph{unknown} and stay
steadier than any single scan.

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.32\textwidth}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.30\textwidth]{\dummyfig}
  \caption{Ray cast versus BFS to the wall.}
  \label{fig:raycast}
\end{wrapfigure}

To pick the right wall we cast a \textbf{ray} from the robot toward the face and take the
first free to occupied transition as its surface (Figure~\ref{fig:raycast}). A breadth
first search would be wrong here: if the face sits just past the wall, the nearest
occupied cell can be the far side of that same wall, while the ray always hits the side
the robot will see.

We fit the 7 nearest wall cells with \textbf{RANSAC}, which shrugs off the stray cells at
a corner, and get a wall direction $\mathbf d=(d_x,d_y)$. The 2D normal is the in plane
perpendicular, from a cross product against the vertical axis $\hat{\mathbf z}$:
\[ \mathbf n = \mathbf d \times \hat{\mathbf z} = (d_y,\,-d_x,\,0). \]
Dropping the zero component leaves $\mathbf n=(d_y,-d_x)$, flipped to point out of the
wall toward the robot.

\paragraph{Goal.}
The stopping \textbf{goal} pushes the face point out along this normal, facing back into
the wall:
\[ \mathbf p_{\text{goal}} = \mathbf p_{\text{face}} + d_{\text{stop}}\,\hat{\mathbf n},
\qquad \theta_{\text{goal}} = \operatorname{atan2}(-n_y,\,-n_x), \]
where $\mathbf p_{\text{face}}$ is the face position, $d_{\text{stop}}=0.4$\,m is the
stopping distance, and $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is the unit wall normal. The heading
$\theta_{\text{goal}}$ makes the robot arrive looking straight at the person from any
approach angle.

\subsection{Face Classification}

Face classification was used after the robot had already detected and approached a person. The goal of this module was not to estimate the person's position, but to identify which known person was in front of the robot and to retrieve their role and gender information for the later decision logic and conversation.

The method is based on face embeddings. A face embedding is a compact numerical representation of a face image. Images of the same person should produce similar embeddings, while images of different people should be farther apart in the embedding space. In our implementation, a FaceNet-style model, \texttt{InceptionResnetV1} pretrained on VGGFace2, is used to compute these embeddings.

For classification, the embedding computed from the live camera image is compared with precomputed embeddings stored in the personnel database. The nearest stored embedding is selected using Euclidean distance. The selected entry gives the person's name, role, and gender.

\subsubsection{Computing Inspection Goals}\label{sec:barrelgoals}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.33\textwidth}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.31\textwidth]{\dummyfig}
  \caption{Inspection goals around a horizontal barrel.}
  \label{fig:barrel_goals}
\end{wrapfigure}

A leak shows up on the \textbf{top or bottom face} of a horizontal barrel, so the robot
needs a clean view of one of them. Seen from above the barrel is symmetric about both
axes, and we know its orientation only up to that symmetry. That is fine: it leaves four
equally good viewing spots, one off each corner, each looking straight at a top or
bottom face (Figure~\ref{fig:barrel_goals}).

\paragraph{Four goals.}
We already have the barrel center and orientation from detection, so we build the four
goals by shifting the center along the orientation vector and the vector perpendicular to
it:
\[ \mathbf p_{ij} = \mathbf c + i\,a\,\hat{\mathbf u} + j\,b\,\hat{\mathbf v},
\qquad i,j \in \{+1,-1\}, \]
with $\mathbf c$ the center, $\hat{\mathbf u}$ the unit orientation along the barrel
axis, $\hat{\mathbf v}$ the unit perpendicular, and $a, b$ the offsets along and across.
At each goal the robot faces the center with an adjustable offset $\varphi$ so the camera
lines up with a top or bottom face:
\[ \theta_{ij} = \operatorname{atan2}(c_y - p_{ij,y},\, c_x - p_{ij,x}) + \varphi. \]

\paragraph{Pruning.}
Some goals land inside a wall or too close to one to be useful. For each goal we measure
the distance to the nearest wall and drop it if that distance is too small or the goal
sits in a wall. The robot visits whatever survives.

\subsection{Anomaly Detection}

\textbf{SuperSimpleNet} gives us both an image level anomaly score and a pixel level
anomaly map: the score decides whether a tile is defective, the map shows where. We
trained it fully supervised, since the dataset shipped with anomaly masks, so the model
learned the defect location directly, which the report needs as a mask.

The first model was trained on the raw dataset. In Gazebo the simulated lighting made the
ceramic tiles much brighter than the training images, a \textbf{domain shift} that hurt
detection. We brightened the tile images into an augmented dataset, retrained from the
existing model, and the augmented model held up under the simulator's lighting. That is
the model the node runs.

\subsubsection{Navigating Along the Belt}\label{sec:belt}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.32\textwidth}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.30\textwidth]{\dummyfig}
  \caption{Line fit to the left scan cone.}
  \label{fig:belt}
\end{wrapfigure}

Along the belt we publish velocities straight to \path|/cmd_vel| and skip nav2. This
close to the wall, localization drifts, so a map pose would be unreliable; instead we
read the belt straight from the \textbf{lidar}, which stays accurate at short range.

\paragraph{Line fit.}
nav2 brings the robot to a fixed keypoint at the head of the belt. From there we fit a
line with \textbf{RANSAC} to the 90 degree scan cone on the robot's left, since the belt
runs along that side (Figure~\ref{fig:belt}). The fit hands back the perpendicular
distance to the belt and the belt's orientation in the robot frame, which we use to set
the exact distance from the belt and to turn precisely perpendicular before the run.

\paragraph{PD wall following.}
We poll the distance every controller tick and steer with a PD law:
\[ e = d_{\text{true}} - d_{\text{target}},
\qquad \omega = K_P\, e + K_D\, \frac{e - e_{\text{prev}}}{\Delta t}, \]
where $d_{\text{true}}$ is the measured perpendicular distance, $d_{\text{target}}$ the
distance we want to hold, $e_{\text{prev}}$ the previous tick's error, $\Delta t$ the
tick period, and $K_P, K_D$ the gains. When the robot drifts away from the belt
$d_{\text{true}}$ grows past $d_{\text{target}}$, the error turns positive, and $\omega$
steers it back toward the belt; drift too close and the error goes negative and pushes it
out again. The D term damps the correction, so the robot holds a straight line instead of
weaving. We call the anomaly service at fixed distance steps as we move down the belt.

\subsection{Follow Line}\label{sec:followline}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.32\textwidth}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.30\textwidth]{\dummyfig}
  \caption{Two sample rows; the line mask is drawn in black.}
  \label{fig:line_rows}
\end{wrapfigure}

The top camera is raised to look down at the floor just in front of the robot. The robot
drives along the line, and at a crossroad it always turns right. When the line ends or an
obstacle blocks the way ahead, it turns around and drives back.

\paragraph{Keying the line.}
We key the \textbf{blue} of the line on two rows of the image, one close to the robot and
one further ahead (Figure~\ref{fig:line_rows}, where the keyed line is drawn in black).
To make the mask robust we apply a \emph{closing}, a dilation followed by an erosion,
which fills small gaps and leaves one solid segment per row.

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.32\textwidth}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.30\textwidth]{\dummyfig}
  \caption{Right edge holds as the line splits (line in black).}
  \label{fig:v_right}
\end{wrapfigure}

\paragraph{Steering with PD.}
We steer with a PD law whose terms come from the image:
\[ e_P = c - x_{\text{near}},
\qquad e_D = x^{R}_{\text{near}} - x^{R}_{\text{far}},
\qquad \omega = K_P\, e_P + K_D\, e_D, \]
where $c$ is the image center column, $x_{\text{near}}$ is the center of the followed
segment on the near row, and $x^{R}_{\text{near}}, x^{R}_{\text{far}}$ are the right edges
of that segment on the near and far rows. The idea matches the belt controller, so we do
not repeat it.

\paragraph{Why the right edge.}
With the P term alone the robot jumps a little at every crossroad and never drives
smoothly, which is why we added the D term. But the D term has to be read carefully. At a
crossroad the line first thickens and then splits in two. Measured from the segment
centers, the center jumps the moment the front line splits from one thick blob into two,
which is exactly the jump we were removing. The \textbf{right edge} does not move when the
line splits (Figure~\ref{fig:v_right}), so tracking it keeps the D term smooth.

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.32\textwidth}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.30\textwidth]{\dummyfig}
  \caption{Approaching a junction from the top (line in black).}
  \label{fig:v_top}
\end{wrapfigure}

\paragraph{Junctions from the top.}
Approaching a junction from the top, the other arm shows up on the robot's right
(Figure~\ref{fig:v_top}). Since the robot follows the rightmost line, it would snap into a
sharp right turn. We reject that arm by requiring a newly detected segment to stay within
$n$ pixels of the segment we are already following.

\paragraph{Obstacles ahead.}
We also watch the lidar like a \textbf{sonar}. We split the 90 degree cone in front of the
robot into 7 bins and average the range in each. If any bin reads too close, the robot
turns around.

\subsection{Navigation Around Map and Yellow Line}\label{sec:navmap}

The robot moves around the first room through a predefined set of poses, each with a
position and an orientation, driven by nav2. Getting it smooth took some tuning, so it is
worth a quick look at how the nav2 controller actually drives.

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.32\textwidth}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.30\textwidth]{\dummyfig}
  \caption{Carrot on the planned path.}
  \label{fig:carrot}
\end{wrapfigure}

\paragraph{Carrots and candidate paths.}
The controller samples several candidate trajectories on each cycle, seeded from the
previous path so it never scores complete nonsense, and the cost function picks the best
one. The robot does not track that path exactly, since it jitters slightly between
samples. Instead it chases a \textbf{carrot} placed on the path at a fixed lookahead
distance (Figure~\ref{fig:carrot}), which keeps the motion smooth.

\paragraph{Inflation and braking.}
The robot kept braking hard near the inflated walls in the costmap, and worse, if it
strayed onto the inflated area by mistake it refused to drive forward and got stuck. Our
localization was accurate, so we turned off the nav2 collision checking and shrank the
inflation a lot, which removed both problems without real risk.

\paragraph{Carrot distance.}
The lookahead was also too long. In tight spaces the carrot pulled the robot over the
inflated edge instead of hugging the path, which was the other reason it got stuck in
corners. We made the lookahead \textbf{scale with speed}: slow means follow the path
almost exactly, fast means more room to maneuver. The shorter carrot at low speed cleared
the tight corners.

\paragraph{Keep out zones.}
The yellow no go zone needed a real mechanism. We built a second map, shaped like the
navigation map, that marks everything inside the yellow lines as a \textbf{keep out zone},
and fed it into the cost function. Every planned path then routes around the keep out
areas on its own.

\subsection{Decision Logic}\label{sec:decision}

For the first task a small \textbf{state machine} was enough, with three states:
\texttt{FollowPath}, \texttt{VisitSubject} and \texttt{GreetSubject}. The second task is
far more tangled, and a flat state machine stops being readable, so we moved to
\texttt{py\_trees\_ros} and built a \textbf{behaviour tree}.

\paragraph{Behaviours.}
A behaviour is a leaf of the tree. On every tick (10\,Hz) it does a slice of work and
returns one of three results: \emph{Running} if it needs more ticks, \emph{Success} if it
is done, or \emph{Failure} if it cannot finish.

\paragraph{Sequences.}
A sequence is an inner node holding an ordered list of children. It runs the first child,
and only when that child returns \emph{Success} does it move to the next; a
\emph{Failure} stops the whole sequence there. A sequence can hold behaviours or other
sequences, so the tree is built by nesting sequences of behaviours. A memory flag decides
whether a sequence re-checks its children from the start on every tick or resumes where it
left off.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{\dummyfig}
  \caption{Example sequence: visiting a detected face.}
  \label{fig:btree}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why it helped.}
This made debugging direct: a failure points at the exact behaviour that caused it. It
also made the code reusable, since many sequences share the same ``go to position X''
behaviours, so we wrote each piece once and tested it everywhere. As an example, the
sequence that visits a detected face chains three behaviours: drive to the goal from the
wall normal, run the conversation, and mark the chosen task active
(Figure~\ref{fig:btree}).

\section{Implementation and Integration}

\subsection{ROS Nodes Architecture}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{\dummyfig}
  \caption{The ROS node and topic graph, from \texttt{rqt\_graph}.}
  \label{fig:rqt}
\end{figure}

The \textbf{movement} node is the central controller and holds the decision logic of
Section~\ref{sec:decision}; every other node feeds it or serves it. Figure~\ref{fig:rqt}
shows the full graph.

\paragraph{Perception nodes.}
\texttt{detect\_faces} publishes faces on \path|/face_detect|, \texttt{detect\_rings}
publishes rings on \path|/ring_detect|, the barrel detector publishes \texttt{BarrelDetect}
messages, and \texttt{anomaly\_detection} exposes the \texttt{detect\_anomalies} service.

\paragraph{Geometry services.}
\texttt{map\_query} serves \texttt{wall\_normal\_at} for faces and \texttt{nearest\_wall}
for barrel goals, \texttt{line\_fit} serves \texttt{line\_fit\_in\_direction} for the
belt, and \texttt{blue\_line} publishes \texttt{BlueLineStatus} on \path|/blue_line|.

\paragraph{Interaction.}
\texttt{conversation} serves \texttt{converse\_person}, \texttt{classify\_face} serves
\texttt{classify\_face}, and \texttt{speak} listens on \path|/speak| and voices whatever
is published there.

\subsection{Face Detection}\label{sec:impl_face}

\textbf{detect\_faces} subscribes to the synchronized OAK-D RGB image and depth point
cloud, so each pair shares a moment in time. It runs YOLO on the RGB image, takes the
center pixel of each box, looks that pixel up in the organized point cloud for a 3D point
in the camera frame, then wraps it in a \texttt{PoseStamped} and transforms it to the
\texttt{map} frame with TF2.

To avoid publishing noise, the node holds new detections as candidates and keeps a
\textbf{running average} of each position. A candidate becomes a real face only after it
has been seen past an acceptance threshold, at which point it gets an id. Accepted faces
are published on \path|/face_detect| as \texttt{FaceDetect}:
\begin{verbatim}
float32 x
float32 y
int8 id
\end{verbatim}

\paragraph{Visiting and greeting.}
\textbf{movement} subscribes to \path|/face_detect| and queues each new id. To visit one
it calls \texttt{wall\_normal\_at} on \texttt{map\_query}, passing the face and robot
positions; the service returns the wall point and its normal
(Section~\ref{sec:facedest}). movement turns those into the 40\,cm stopping goal, sends it
to nav2, and on arrival calls \texttt{converse\_person} on \texttt{conversation}. That
service takes the person's gender and returns the chosen \textbf{task} string, which
movement marks active.

\paragraph{Re-driving and fallback.}
If \texttt{detect\_faces} republishes the same id, the true face has moved, so movement
recomputes the goal from the new position. If \texttt{wall\_normal\_at} reports no usable
wall, movement falls back to the direct robot to face approach from
Section~\ref{sec:facedest}.

\subsection{Face Classification}

The face classification module is implemented as the ROS 2 node \texttt{classify\_face}. It exposes the service \texttt{/classify\_face}, which is called by the central movement module when the robot is close enough to a detected person.

The node subscribes to the OAK-D RGB image topic \path|/oakd/rgb/preview/image_raw| and keeps the latest frame. When the service is called, it collects several fresh frames, with ten samples used by default. For each frame, the image is converted to OpenCV format and YOLO is run with class \texttt{0}, corresponding to a person. The first detected bounding box is cropped from the image and used as the input for face embedding.

The crop is passed through \texttt{InceptionResnetV1}. The resulting embedding is compared to all entries in \texttt{personnel\_embeddings\_dict.py}. The nearest match is selected using Euclidean distance.

To make the result more stable, the module does not rely on only one frame. It repeats the detection and embedding process over multiple samples and stores a vote for the nearest matching person in each frame. The final identity is chosen by majority vote. If no person is detected in the first frame, or if no valid match is found in any frame, the service returns \texttt{success = false}.

On success, the service returns the matched name, role, gender, and a message describing how many samples voted for the selected identity. The movement module stores this information in the current pending person and updates the visualization label.

\subsection{Ring Detection}

\textbf{detect\_rings} publishes each ring on \path|/ring_detect| as \texttt{RingDetect},
which carries the position, an id and a color. movement does little at drive time: it
records where each ring was seen and its color, and keeps them for the report.

\subsection{Barrel Detection}

The barrel detector publishes \texttt{BarrelDetect} for every barrel it sees, whether or
not a task is active. The message carries the position and normal, an id, the color, a
horizontal flag, a leaking flag and a path to a saved image.

\paragraph{Visiting horizontal barrels.}
Once a person sets the barrel task active, movement visits every horizontal barrel it has
stored, and any new one it later sees. For each candidate goal around a barrel
(Section~\ref{sec:barrelgoals}) it calls \texttt{nearest\_wall} on \texttt{map\_query},
which returns the closest wall point; movement keeps the goal only if that point is far
enough away. It then drives to a surviving goal with nav2.

\paragraph{Announcing a leak.}
On arrival the detector re-sends a \texttt{BarrelDetect} with the leaking flag set if the
barrel is leaking, with no service call needed. When movement sees that flag it publishes
to \path|/speak| and the \texttt{speak} node says the warning out loud. The image path is
stored for the report.

\subsection{Anomaly Detection}

When a person sets a red or green anomaly task active, movement drives to a fixed keypoint
at the belt with nav2, then takes over on \path|/cmd_vel|: it uses the lidar line fit to
set its distance and turn perpendicular (Section~\ref{sec:belt}), drives down the belt,
and calls \texttt{detect\_anomalies} every fixed step. Each call returns a result and the
paths to a saved tile image and mask, which movement stores per tile for the report.

\paragraph{The detector.}
\textbf{anomaly\_detection} keeps the latest top camera frame and acts only when its
\texttt{detect\_anomalies} service is called. It first finds the \textbf{tile}: grayscale,
a fixed intensity threshold, a morphological closing to fill gaps, and a border strip
removed so edge objects are ignored. It takes the largest contour, its convex hull, and
approximates four corners with \texttt{approxPolyDP}. A \textbf{homography} then warps that
quad to a square, so the model always sees the tile head on, and LAB brightness
normalization evens out lighting. The square goes to SuperSimpleNet, and the score against
a threshold decides \texttt{defected}, \texttt{not\_defected}, or \texttt{not\_found} when
no tile is found. The response carries the saved tile and mask paths.

\subsection{Line Following}

\textbf{blue\_line} does the vision and publishes \texttt{BlueLineStatus} on
\path|/blue_line|: a state that is \texttt{LOST}, \texttt{LINE} or \texttt{CROSSROAD},
with the line's left and right offsets. movement turns the offsets into the P and D terms
of Section~\ref{sec:followline}, turns right on \texttt{CROSSROAD}, and turns around on
\texttt{LOST} or when the obstacle bins read too close.

\paragraph{Finding the CTO.}
The one step specific to this task is the stop condition. When the line is lost or an
obstacle blocks the way, movement calls \texttt{classify\_face} on \texttt{classify\_face},
which grabs a live frame and returns the person's name, role and gender. If the role is
the CTO, the robot has arrived and report generation fires; otherwise it keeps following
the line.

\subsection{Speech and Remaining Services}

\textbf{speak} is a shared utility: it listens on \path|/speak| and voices whatever is
published there, which the greeting, the leak alert and the task prompts all use. The
\texttt{line\_fit} node's \texttt{line\_fit\_in\_direction} service, used for the belt,
fits a line to a lidar cone in a given direction and returns the perpendicular distance
and the rotation needed to face it square on.
